The Art of the Critical Essay: Why Film Criticism Is More Than a Star Rating

2026-07-28 · Industry · Tags: The, moviereviewhub, industry

In an era where a single scroll through a streaming platform yields a cacophony of user scores, aggregate percentages, and binary thumbs, the role of the film critic has never been more misunderstood—or more vital. We are drowning in data, yet starving for meaning. The star rating, once a helpful shorthand, has become a reductive currency that flattens the complex, visceral, and intellectual experience of cinema into a simple buy-or-skip verdict. This article is a call to step back from the algorithmic noise and rediscover the lost art of the critical essay—a form of writing that treats film not as a product to be rated, but as a text to be interrogated, contextualized, and celebrated. As a critic, my job is not to tell you whether a movie is 'good' or 'bad,' but to illuminate how it works, why it was made, and what it says about the world we inhabit. Let's explore why the critical essay is the most potent tool we have for understanding cinema, and how you, as a viewer, can cultivate a deeper appreciation for the art form by engaging with criticism on a more profound level.

The Death of the Star and the Rise of the Hot Take

The modern film discourse is dominated by the 'hot take'—a pithy, often reactionary opinion designed to generate clicks and social media engagement. It is the cinematic equivalent of fast food: satisfying in the moment, but nutritionally void. The hot take thrives on absolutes: 'This is a masterpiece,' or 'This is a disaster.' It leaves no room for nuance, for the messy middle ground where most interesting art actually resides. The star rating system, while convenient, is complicit in this dumbing-down. It reduces a filmmaker's years of labor, a cast's collective performance, and a writer's intricate narrative architecture to a single, arbitrary number.

This is not to say that all star ratings are useless. They can serve as a quick filter for casual viewers. But the problem arises when we mistake the rating for the criticism itself. A rating is a conclusion, not an analysis. It is the final sentence of an essay, stripped of the preceding paragraphs that give it context and weight. When we rely solely on ratings, we lose the ability to articulate *why* a film moved us, angered us, or left us cold. We lose the language to discuss the subtle interplay of light and shadow, the rhythm of the edit, the subtext buried in a line of dialogue. The critical essay, in contrast, is a journey. It invites the reader to walk alongside the critic as they unpack a film's themes, techniques, and cultural significance. It is a collaborative act of discovery, not a pronouncement from on high.

Context Is King: Placing a Film in the World

A great critical essay does not exist in a vacuum. It understands that a film is not born in a sterile laboratory but emerges from a specific historical, cultural, and industrial moment. Consider the neo-noir thrillers of the 1970s, like Chinatown or The Long Goodbye. To review them solely on their plot mechanics is to miss the point entirely. These films are deeply cynical responses to the political disillusionment of the post-Watergate era. Their murky moralities and downbeat endings are not flaws; they are reflections of a nation losing faith in its institutions. A critic who ignores this context is doing the reader a disservice, presenting a two-dimensional view of a three-dimensional object.

This contextual approach is even more critical when examining international cinema. A film from South Korea, like Bong Joon-ho's Parasite, is not just a clever thriller about class conflict; it is a searing indictment of specific socioeconomic realities in South Korea, wrapped in a genre package that makes it palatable to global audiences. Without understanding the Korean concept of han (a feeling of unresolved resentment and grief) or the country's hyper-competitive housing market, a Western viewer might appreciate the film's twists but miss its profound emotional core. The critical essay bridges this gap. It acts as a translator, not just of language, but of culture, history, and social context. It enriches the viewing experience by providing the tools necessary to see a film through the eyes of its original audience.

The Craft of the Frame: Analyzing Technique and Form

Beyond context, the critical essay is the primary vehicle for discussing the actual craft of filmmaking. This is where we move from what a film is about to *how* it is about it. A film's meaning is not just in its story; it is in its form. The long, unbroken take in a film like 1917 is not a gimmick; it is a philosophical statement about the inescapable, continuous nature of war. The rapid-fire montage in a Soviet propaganda film is not just an editing style; it is a tool for shaping ideology. The use of deep focus in Citizen Kane is not just a technical achievement; it is a way of showing the simultaneous isolation and power of Charles Foster Kane.

When a critic writes about mise-en-scène, cinematography, sound design, and editing, they are not being pedantic. They are revealing the invisible machinery that generates emotion and meaning. For example, consider the use of color in a film like The Handmaiden. The shift from muted, grey tones in the Japanese colonial era to the vibrant, saturated colors of the lovers' escape is not just a visual treat; it is a narrative device that signals a shift from oppression to liberation. A star rating can tell you the film is 'beautiful,' but only a critical essay can explain *why* that beauty is essential to the film's argument. By focusing on technique, the critic empowers the viewer to see films with a more discerning eye, transforming them from passive consumers into active readers of the visual language.

Beyond the Verdict: The Essay as a Conversation

The most compelling critical essays are not definitive; they are conversational. They pose questions, acknowledge ambiguities, and even admit to personal biases. The best critics, like the late Roger Ebert, understood that a review is not a final judgment but an opening salvo in a larger conversation. Ebert's reviews were personal, passionate, and often contradictory, but they always invited the reader to engage with the film on their own terms. He famously wrote about The Godfather not as a perfect object, but as a film that 'fires on all cylinders,' a phrase that suggests a dynamic, ongoing process rather than a static conclusion.

This conversational approach is more important than ever in our fragmented media landscape. A critical essay should be a starting point for discussion, not an end point. It should give you the vocabulary and the framework to articulate your own reactions, even if they differ from the critic's. When a critic writes, 'This film made me uncomfortable, and here is why,' they are not just sharing a feeling; they are inviting you to examine your own discomfort. This is the antithesis of the algorithmic recommendation, which seeks to confirm your existing tastes. A great critical essay challenges you, provokes you, and sometimes even angers you. But it never leaves you indifferent. It treats you as an intelligent, thinking partner in the cinematic experience.

How to Read a Review: A Guide for the Discerning Viewer

So, how can you, as a viewer, get the most out of film criticism? First, seek out critics who have a distinct point of view and a deep knowledge of film history. Avoid critics who seem to be writing for a press release or who are simply chasing trends. Look for writers who can articulate *why* a film works or fails, using specific examples from the screen. Second, read reviews *after* you have seen the film, not before. This allows you to form your own initial impressions and then test them against the critic's analysis. You will be surprised how often a good critic can illuminate a detail you missed or offer a new interpretation of a scene you thought you understood.

Third, and most importantly, do not be afraid to disagree. A review is not a command; it is a perspective. If a critic pans a film you loved, ask yourself why. Did they miss the point? Or are they seeing something you didn't? This process of intellectual friction is where real growth happens. Finally, try writing your own critical essays, even if they are just for yourself. You do not need to be a professional to analyze a film. After your next movie night, take ten minutes to write down your thoughts. What was the film trying to say? How did it use its technical elements to achieve that goal? What context did it bring to the table? You will quickly discover that the act of writing about a film deepens your appreciation for it, forcing you to move beyond a simple 'I liked it' or 'I hated it.'

The Future of Criticism in a Post-Algorithm World

As artificial intelligence and recommendation algorithms become more sophisticated, the value of human criticism will only increase. An algorithm can tell you what you are statistically likely to enjoy based on your past behavior, but it cannot tell you what you *need* to see. It cannot challenge your worldview, introduce you to a perspective you have never considered, or explain the historical significance of a film that is outside your usual genre preferences. The algorithm is a mirror, reflecting your own tastes back at you. The critical essay is a window, opening onto a vast and varied landscape of human expression.

The future of film criticism lies not in competing with the algorithm, but in offering something it fundamentally cannot: a humanistic, contextual, and deeply personal engagement with art. It lies in the long-form essay, the video analysis, the podcast deep-dive—all formats that allow for nuance, complexity, and the messy, beautiful process of thinking out loud. As streaming platforms continue to churn out content at an unprecedented rate, the role of the critic as a curator and interpreter becomes even more essential. We are not gatekeepers; we are guides. We are not judges; we are interpreters. We are here to help you navigate the endless sea of content, not by giving you a simple thumbs up or down, but by giving you the tools to see the world through a new lens.

Conclusion

The next time you find yourself staring at a star rating, remember that it is merely a shadow of a much richer conversation. The true art of film criticism lies in the essay—a space where context, craft, and personal reflection converge to create a deeper understanding of the moving image. It is a practice that enriches not only the way we watch movies, but the way we see the world. So, the next time a film leaves you speechless, do not just rate it. Write about it. Talk about it. Argue about it. Engage with the criticism of others. In doing so, you will not only become a more discerning viewer, but a more active participant in the ongoing, ever-evolving dialogue that is cinema. Let us move beyond the verdict and embrace the journey.
