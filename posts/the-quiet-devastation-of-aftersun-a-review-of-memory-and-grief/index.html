The Quiet Devastation of 'Aftersun': A Review of Memory and Grief

2026-07-26 · Reviews · Tags: The, moviereviewhub, reviews

In an era of blockbuster spectacle and algorithmic storytelling, it takes a special film to remind us that cinema's greatest power lies in its ability to capture the ineffable. Charlotte Wells's debut feature, Aftersun (2022), is precisely such a film—a quiet, devastating masterpiece that lingers long after the credits roll. It is not a film that shouts; it whispers, and in that whisper, it reveals the profound depths of memory, grief, and the fragile bond between a parent and child. As a critic, I've seen hundreds of films, but few have left me as breathless and introspective as this one. This review explores why Aftersun is not just a great film, but an essential piece of cinema for our times.

The Fragile Fabric of Memory

At its core, Aftersun is a memory piece. The narrative unfolds through the eyes of Sophie, an adult woman (played with haunting restraint by Celia Rowlson-Hall) who revisits a camcorder recording of a holiday she took with her father, Calum (Paul Mescal), twenty years earlier. The film interweaves these grainy, nostalgic VHS tapes with more polished, present-day shots, creating a dual timeline that feels both intimate and disorienting. Wells masterfully captures how memory is not a linear record but a collage of fragments—some sharp, some blurred, some entirely invented by our minds to fill the gaps.

What makes this exploration so effective is its refusal to explain. We never learn exactly what happened to Calum after that holiday, nor do we get a tidy resolution. Instead, we are left with the emotional truth of the experience. The film trusts its audience to sit with ambiguity, to feel the weight of what is left unsaid. In a world where every plot point is often over-explained, Aftersun stands as a testament to the power of suggestion. It reminds us that the most profound memories are often the ones we cannot fully articulate.

The Performances That Anchor the Film

Paul Mescal delivers a career-defining performance as Calum, a young father grappling with depression and a sense of inadequacy. Mescal's portrayal is a masterclass in subtlety—he conveys a world of pain through a fleeting glance, a hesitant smile, or a moment of stillness. His Calum is both loving and distant, present and absent, a man trying to be the father he thinks his daughter deserves while drowning in his own internal turmoil. The way Mescal allows vulnerability to seep through his stoic exterior is nothing short of breathtaking.

Opposite him, newcomer Frankie Corio as young Sophie is a revelation. Corio brings a naturalistic charm and curiosity to the role, capturing the innocence of childhood while hinting at a deeper awareness of her father's fragility. The chemistry between the two is palpable; their interactions feel unscripted and genuine, as if we are watching a real father-daughter duo on holiday. It is this authenticity that makes the film's emotional payoff so devastating. When the final scene arrives—a moment that recontextualizes everything we've seen—it is the performances that make it land with the force of a gut punch.

Visual Language and Sound Design

Wells, who also wrote the screenplay, demonstrates a remarkable command of visual storytelling. The film's cinematography, by Gregory Oke, is a study in contrasts: the sun-drenched, saturated colors of the Turkish resort where the holiday takes place, juxtaposed with the muted, desaturated tones of the present-day scenes. This visual dichotomy mirrors the way memory can both idealize and distort the past. The use of handheld cameras and long takes creates an intimate, almost documentary-like feel, drawing us into the characters' world.

Equally important is the sound design. The film is punctuated by moments of silence that speak volumes, and the ambient sounds of the resort—waves crashing, distant laughter, the hum of a karaoke machine—create a rich auditory tapestry. The soundtrack, featuring songs like Blur's "Tender" and Queen's "Under Pressure," is used sparingly but effectively, each track carrying emotional weight. The climactic use of "Under Pressure" in a karaoke scene is a masterstroke, transforming a pop anthem into a heartbreaking duet between father and daughter, a moment of connection that is both joyful and unbearably sad.

The Unspoken Grief

What truly sets Aftersun apart is its handling of grief. The film never explicitly states that Calum is struggling with suicidal thoughts, but the subtext is unmistakable. Through a series of subtle cues—a late-night swim, a moment of panic in a crowded market, a tearful phone call—Wells paints a portrait of a man on the edge. The film's genius lies in its refusal to confirm or deny these interpretations, allowing each viewer to project their own experiences onto the narrative. This ambiguity is not a flaw but a feature, making the film deeply personal and cathartic.

For those who have lost a loved one to mental illness or suicide, Aftersun will resonate on a visceral level. It captures the way grief can resurface years later, triggered by a song, a photograph, or a memory. The film's final sequence, in which adult Sophie watches the camcorder footage and sees her father's pain through new eyes, is a devastating reminder that we often only understand our parents' struggles in hindsight. It is a film about the stories we tell ourselves to make sense of loss, and the ways in which love and grief are inextricably intertwined.

Why It Resonates in 2026

In 2026, as we navigate an increasingly fragmented and fast-paced world, Aftersun feels more relevant than ever. We are bombarded with content that demands our attention, yet rarely offers the space for quiet reflection. This film is an antidote to that noise. It asks us to slow down, to sit with discomfort, and to engage with the complexities of human emotion. In an age of AI-generated scripts and formulaic blockbusters, Aftersun stands as a testament to the power of personal, auteur-driven cinema.

Moreover, the film's exploration of mental health and fatherhood is a conversation we desperately need to have. As societal pressures continue to mount, the silent struggles of men are often overlooked. Aftersun offers a compassionate, unflinching look at these issues, encouraging empathy and understanding. It is a film that not only entertains but also educates, making it a valuable addition to any cinephile's watchlist.

Conclusion

Aftersun is not an easy film to watch, but it is an essential one. It is a work of profound beauty and emotional intelligence, a film that rewards patience and introspection. Charlotte Wells has crafted a debut that announces her as a major talent, and the performances from Mescal and Corio are among the best of the decade. This is a film that will stay with you, haunting your thoughts and reshaping the way you view your own memories. If you haven't seen it yet, do yourself a favor—find a quiet evening, dim the lights, and let Aftersun wash over you. It is a masterpiece that deserves to be seen, discussed, and cherished.
