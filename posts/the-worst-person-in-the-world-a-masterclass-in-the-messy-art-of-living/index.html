The Worst Person in the World: A Masterclass in the Messy Art of Living

2026-07-30 · Reviews · Tags: reviews, The

Every once in a while, a film arrives that feels less like a story and more like a mirror held up to the chaos of modern existence. Joachim Trier's The Worst Person in the World (2021) is precisely that—a tender, witty, and achingly honest portrait of a young woman stumbling through the fog of her own potential. It is not a film about finding answers, but about the courage to keep asking questions. As a critic, I've seen hundreds of coming-of-age tales, but this one cuts deeper because it refuses to romanticize growth. Instead, it celebrates the messiness, the wrong turns, and the quiet moments of clarity that define a life. In this review, I'll dissect why this film is a modern masterpiece, explore its narrative brilliance, and offer a guide to where you can stream it and what to watch next.

The Architecture of Uncertainty: How the Film's Structure Mirrors Life

Trier and his co-writer Eskil Vogt structure the film as a series of twelve chapters, each with a title that reads like a short story heading—'The Worst Person in the World,' 'The New Boyfriend,' 'The Time Freeze.' This episodic approach is not a gimmick; it's a deliberate reflection of how we remember our own lives. We don't experience time as a continuous flow, but as a collection of pivotal moments, missed connections, and sudden shifts. The film jumps forward in time, sometimes by months, sometimes by years, without warning. This disorientation is intentional. It forces us to piece together Julie's journey the way we piece together our own past—through fragments, emotions, and the lingering echo of decisions.

What makes this structure so effective is that it never feels episodic in a lazy sense. Each chapter is a self-contained vignette that advances Julie's internal arc, even when the external plot seems to stall. For instance, the chapter 'The Time Freeze' is a surreal sequence where Julie runs through Oslo while the world stands still, a metaphor for her desire to escape the consequences of her choices. It's a bold, almost magical realist moment that could have felt out of place, but Trier grounds it in Julie's emotional reality. The structure, then, is not just a narrative device; it's a philosophical statement. Life is not a straight line, and neither is this film.

Julie: A Portrait of Flawed Humanity

At the center of this whirlwind is Julie, played with breathtaking vulnerability by Renate Reinsve, who won the Best Actress award at Cannes for her performance. Julie is not a typical protagonist. She is indecisive, impulsive, and often selfish. She abandons a promising medical career to study psychology, then drops that to become a photographer. She leaves a stable, loving relationship with Aksel (Anders Danielsen Lie) for a fleeting connection with Eivind (Herbert Nordrum). She makes mistakes, hurts people, and often doesn't know what she wants. Yet, she is utterly compelling because her flaws are so recognizable. Who hasn't felt the panic of being in your late twenties and still not having a 'life plan'? Who hasn't sabotaged a good thing out of fear of settling?

Reinsve's performance is a masterclass in subtlety. She conveys Julie's inner turmoil through micro-expressions—a twitch of the mouth, a glance away, a nervous laugh. In one scene, she sits on a toilet and takes a pregnancy test, and the camera lingers on her face as she processes the result. No dialogue, no music, just pure emotion. It's a moment that encapsulates the film's power: it trusts the actor and the audience to fill in the gaps. Julie is not 'the worst person in the world'—the title is ironic, a self-deprecating joke she makes early on. But she is human, and that is far more interesting.

Visual Poetry: Trier's Directorial Signature

Joachim Trier has always been a director who understands the visual language of emotion. In The Worst Person in the World, he employs a palette of warm, muted tones—amber, ochre, soft blues—that evoke the melancholic beauty of Oslo. The cinematography, by Kasper Tuxen, is intimate and handheld, often capturing Julie in close-up, as if we are sitting beside her. But Trier also knows when to pull back. The famous 'time freeze' scene is a stunning long take where Julie runs through the city, weaving through frozen pedestrians and cyclists, her hair flowing in the wind. It's a moment of pure cinematic magic, a visual metaphor for her desire to break free from the constraints of time and consequence.

Another striking sequence is the film's opening, which uses a montage of Julie's childhood and adolescence to establish her restless spirit. Trier cuts between home videos, still photographs, and staged scenes, creating a collage of memory that feels both personal and universal. This visual storytelling is not just decorative; it deepens our understanding of Julie's psyche. We see her as a child, already questioning the world, and we understand why she is the way she is. Trier's direction is confident and assured, never flashy but always purposeful. He lets the story breathe, allowing moments of silence to speak louder than words.

Themes That Resonate: Love, Career, and the Fear of Missing Out

At its core, The Worst Person in the World is a meditation on the modern condition. It explores the tension between ambition and contentment, between the desire for freedom and the need for connection. Julie's relationships with Aksel and Eivind represent two poles: Aksel is older, established, and ready to settle down; Eivind is spontaneous, playful, and equally uncertain about the future. Julie oscillates between them, never fully committing to either. This is not a love triangle in the traditional sense; it's a reflection of the paradox of choice that plagues our generation. We are told we can have it all, but the more options we have, the more anxious we become.

The film also tackles the fear of missing out (FOMO) with devastating accuracy. Julie is constantly looking over her shoulder, wondering if the grass is greener elsewhere. She leaves Aksel because she feels she's missing out on the excitement of new love, only to realize that Eivind is not the answer either. In one poignant scene, she visits Aksel in the hospital as he is dying of cancer, and they have a conversation that is both heartbreaking and cathartic. Aksel tells her, 'You are the worst person in the world, but you are also the best.' It's a line that captures the film's central paradox: we are all flawed, but we are also capable of profound love and growth. The film doesn't offer easy resolutions; it simply shows that life is a series of imperfect choices, and that's okay.

Where to Watch and What to Watch Next

If you haven't seen The Worst Person in the World yet, it's currently streaming on Hulu in the United States, and available on MUBI in several international markets. It's also available for rental on Amazon Prime Video, Apple TV, and Vudu. Given its critical acclaim, it's a film that deserves a place on your watchlist, especially if you appreciate character-driven dramas with a touch of whimsy.

If you're looking for similar films that explore the messy complexities of life and love, consider these recommendations:

  • Frances Ha (2012) – Noah Baumbach's black-and-white ode to a struggling dancer in New York, starring Greta Gerwig. It shares the same spirit of youthful indecision and self-discovery.
  • Marriage Story (2019) – Also by Baumbach, this film examines the dissolution of a marriage with raw honesty, featuring career-best performances from Adam Driver and Scarlett Johansson.
  • Certified Copy (2010) – Abbas Kiarostami's philosophical drama about a couple whose relationship blurs the line between reality and fiction, starring Juliette Binoche.
  • Past Lives (2023) – Celine Song's debut about two childhood friends who reunite decades later, exploring the roads not taken. It's a spiritual sibling to Trier's film.

Each of these films, like The Worst Person in the World, refuses to offer easy answers. Instead, they invite you to sit with discomfort and find beauty in the ambiguity.

Conclusion: A Film That Stays With You

The Worst Person in the World is not a film you watch and forget. It lingers in your mind, prompting you to revisit your own choices and the paths you didn't take. It's a rare cinematic achievement that is both deeply personal and universally relatable. Trier and Reinsve have crafted a character who is infuriatingly human, and in doing so, they've created a film that feels like a warm, honest conversation with a close friend. Whether you're in your twenties, thirties, or beyond, you will see a piece of yourself in Julie. And that is the mark of a true masterpiece. So, if you're looking for a film that will make you laugh, cry, and think, look no further. This is essential viewing for anyone who has ever felt lost in the beautiful chaos of being alive.
