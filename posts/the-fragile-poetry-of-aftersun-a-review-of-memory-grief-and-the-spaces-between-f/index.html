The Fragile Poetry of 'Aftersun': A Review of Memory, Grief, and the Spaces Between Frames

2026-08-09 · Reviews · Tags: reviews, moviereviewhub, The

Some films wash over you like a wave, leaving you breathless but intact. Others seep into your bones slowly, like the chill of a sea breeze that lingers long after you've left the shore. Charlotte Wells's debut feature, Aftersun, belongs to the latter category. It's a film that doesn't demand your attention so much as it quietly earns it, unfolding like a half-remembered dream. On the surface, it's a simple story: a young father, Calum (Paul Mescal), takes his 11-year-old daughter, Sophie (Frankie Corio), on a budget holiday to a Turkish resort in the late 1990s. But beneath the sun-drenched surface lies a profound meditation on memory, grief, and the chasm between what we remember and what we can never know. This is not a film that explains itself; it's one that invites you to sit with its ambiguities, to feel its weight, and to leave with more questions than answers. In an era of algorithmic storytelling and spoon-fed narratives, Aftersun is a radical act of trust in the audience's intelligence—and it pays off in ways that are as devastating as they are beautiful.

The Narrative Structure: Memory as a Fragmented Tapestry

Wells constructs Aftersun not as a linear narrative but as a mosaic of moments, each one a shard of a memory that Sophie, now an adult, is trying to piece together. The film opens with grainy, low-resolution footage of Sophie as a child, captured on a camcorder—a device that becomes the film's central metaphor. These home-video snippets are intercut with the present-day, where an adult Sophie (Celia Rowlson-Hall) watches the tapes in a dark room, searching for clues about her father, who is conspicuously absent from her adult life. The narrative doesn't follow a traditional arc; instead, it drifts through time, jumping between the holiday's mundane activities—swimming, eating, playing pool—and more surreal, almost dreamlike sequences that hint at something darker. This fragmented structure mirrors the way memory actually works: not as a chronological timeline but as a series of emotional snapshots, some sharp, some blurred, all tinged with the subjectivity of the rememberer. Wells masterfully avoids the trap of over-explaining. She never tells us what happened to Calum; she simply shows us the holiday through Sophie's eyes, and we are left to infer the tragedy that looms over every frame. This restraint is the film's greatest strength, turning the viewing experience into an active act of excavation. We become detectives, sifting through the visual clues, trying to understand the man who smiles for the camera but whose eyes betray a profound sadness.

The Visual Language: The Camcorder as a Window to the Soul

One of the most striking aspects of Aftersun is its visual duality. Wells alternates between the polished, cinematic 35mm footage that represents Sophie's present-day perspective and the grainy, shaky DV footage that represents the holiday as it was recorded. The camcorder footage is not just a stylistic flourish; it's a narrative device that underscores the film's central theme of perception. When Sophie films her father, she captures only what he allows her to see—the playful dad who does silly dances, the one who teaches her to breathe properly. But the camera also catches moments that Sophie, as a child, might not have understood: Calum standing alone on the balcony at night, his silhouette dissolving into the darkness; Calum crying in the bathroom, his sobs muffled by the running water. These are the moments that adult Sophie revisits, and they are the moments that break our hearts. The DV aesthetic also serves a practical purpose: it places us firmly in the late '90s, a time before smartphones and social media, when a camcorder was the only way to preserve a memory. But Wells uses this technology to explore the gap between the recorded image and the lived experience. The camera lies, or at least it tells a partial truth. It captures the surface, but not the depth. In one of the film's most devastating sequences, Calum dances alone in a dark room, his movements frantic and desperate, while the camera—held by Sophie—captures only a blur of motion. It's a moment that feels almost like an exorcism, and it's one that adult Sophie will never fully understand. The visual language of Aftersun is a masterclass in showing, not telling, and it's a testament to Wells's directorial vision that she can evoke such profound emotion from a simple shift in aspect ratio and grain.

The Performances: A Duet of Unspoken Emotion

At the heart of Aftersun are two performances that are nothing short of revelatory. Paul Mescal, who burst onto the scene with Normal People, delivers a career-defining turn as Calum. He embodies the character with a quiet intensity that is both magnetic and heartbreaking. Calum is a man who is trying his best to be a good father, but he's clearly struggling with his own demons. Mescal conveys this through the smallest gestures—a flicker of his eyes, a tightening of his jaw, a pause before he answers a question. He never overplays the sadness; instead, he lets it simmer beneath the surface, occasionally breaking through in moments of unexpected vulnerability. One scene, in particular, stands out: Calum and Sophie are at a karaoke bar, and he sings a painfully off-key rendition of "Losing My Religion." It's a moment of pure, unguarded emotion, and Mescal's raw delivery is so authentic that it feels like we're intruding on a private moment. Opposite him, Frankie Corio, in her film debut, is a revelation. She captures the awkwardness and curiosity of an 11-year-old with remarkable naturalism. Sophie is not a precocious child; she's just a kid trying to understand her father, and Corio's performance is full of subtle observations—the way she watches him when he doesn't know she's looking, the way she tries to cheer him up with silly jokes, the way she holds his hand with a tenderness that belies her age. The chemistry between Mescal and Corio is palpable; they feel like a real father and daughter, with all the love, frustration, and unspoken tension that entails. Their relationship is the film's emotional core, and it's a testament to both actors that we feel every ounce of their connection and every hint of the tragedy that awaits.

The Emotional Core: Grief, Depression, and the Unspoken

Aftersun is a film about grief, but it's not the kind of grief that comes from a sudden loss. It's the slow, creeping grief that comes from watching someone you love disappear into themselves. Calum is clearly suffering from depression, though the film never labels it. We see it in his sleepless nights, his distant stares, his occasional outbursts of frustration. He's a man who is barely holding it together, and Sophie, even at 11, senses that something is wrong. She doesn't have the vocabulary to articulate it, but she knows that her father is not okay. The film explores this dynamic with remarkable sensitivity, never resorting to melodrama or easy answers. Instead, it shows us the small ways that depression manifests—the way Calum forgets to pack his medication, the way he gets lost in a book while Sophie plays in the pool, the way he apologizes for being "a bit tired." These are the moments that adult Sophie will later revisit, and they are the moments that will haunt her. The film's title, Aftersun, is itself a metaphor. Just as the sun's rays can burn your skin long after you've left the beach, the effects of a parent's mental illness can linger for a lifetime. Wells doesn't offer any catharsis or resolution; instead, she leaves us with the image of Sophie, now an adult, watching the tapes and trying to find the father she never truly knew. It's a devastating portrait of the ways in which we can never fully know another person, even the ones we love most.

The Soundtrack and Sound Design: Music as Memory

Music plays a crucial role in Aftersun, not just as a backdrop but as a narrative device that bridges the gap between past and present. The film is set in 1999, and the soundtrack is a nostalgic mix of pop hits and indie anthems—Blur's "Tender," Queen's "Under Pressure," and, most memorably, the aforementioned R.E.M. song. These songs are not just period pieces; they are emotional touchstones that evoke the era and the characters' inner lives. Wells uses music to create a sense of temporal dislocation, often letting a song bleed from one scene to the next, blurring the line between memory and reality. The sound design is equally meticulous. The ambient sounds of the resort—the waves, the pool, the distant chatter—create a sense of place that is both idyllic and oppressive. But it's the silences that are most powerful. In the film's final sequence, the sound drops out almost entirely, leaving only the faint hum of the camcorder and the sound of Sophie's breathing. It's a moment of pure, unadulterated emotion, and it's a testament to the power of silence in cinema. Wells understands that sometimes the most profound feelings are the ones that can't be put into words, and she gives us the space to feel them.

Why 'Aftersun' Matters in Today's Cinema

In a cinematic landscape dominated by franchises, sequels, and algorithm-driven content, Aftersun stands as a beacon of what independent cinema can achieve. It's a film that trusts its audience, that doesn't spoon-feed us with exposition or tidy resolutions. It's a film that rewards patience and attention, and it's a film that will stay with you long after the credits roll. Wells's debut is a masterclass in emotional storytelling, and it's a reminder that the most powerful stories are often the quietest ones. Aftersun has been hailed as one of the best films of the decade, and it's easy to see why. It's a film that speaks to the universal experience of loss, of trying to understand the people we love, and of the ways in which memory can both comfort and torment us. It's also a film that showcases the incredible talent of its cast and crew, from Mescal's nuanced performance to Wells's assured direction. In a world that often feels noisy and chaotic, Aftersun offers a moment of quiet reflection—a chance to sit with our own memories and to consider the people we've lost, and the things we never said.

Conclusion

Aftersun is not an easy film to watch. It's slow, it's ambiguous, and it leaves you with a sense of unease that lingers. But it's also a film of profound beauty and emotional depth, one that rewards multiple viewings and invites you to bring your own experiences to the table. Charlotte Wells has crafted a debut that is both deeply personal and universally resonant, and she has done so with a level of skill and maturity that is rare in any filmmaker, let alone a first-time director. If you're looking for a film that will challenge you, move you, and stay with you, Aftersun is essential viewing. It's a reminder that the most important stories are often the ones that are hardest to tell, and that the spaces between frames can hold more meaning than the images themselves. So grab a box of tissues, find a quiet room, and let this film wash over you. You won't regret it.
